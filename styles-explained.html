Yoga styles explained

Hatha, Vinyasa, Yin, Restorative, Iyengar and Ashtanga turn up on almost every studio schedule. Here is what each class actually looks like from the mat: how quickly it moves, how long poses are held, which props come out, and how the hour is put together.

None of these styles is more "real" than another, and none is reserved for a particular kind of body. They are different ways of organizing the same raw material: poses, breath and attention. Start with the comparison table, then read the section for any style that catches your interest.

At a glance

Six styles, side by side

These are typical ranges, not rules. Two teachers of the same style can run very different rooms, so use this as a starting map and read the class description on the schedule too. Select a style name to jump to its section.

How six common yoga styles compare by pace, typical hold time, props and class structure
Style Pace Typical hold Props Class structure
Hatha Slow to moderate, with pauses between poses 3 to 8 breaths, about 20 to 50 seconds Sometimes: blocks, strap, blanket Breath or warm-up, standing poses, floor poses, rest. Varies by teacher.
Vinyasa Moderate to brisk and continuous, one movement per breath 1 to 5 breaths Occasionally: blocks Warm-up, Sun Salutations, a flowing standing sequence toward a peak pose, floor cool-down, rest. Changes every class.
Yin Very slow, mostly still 3 to 5 minutes, sometimes longer Often: bolsters, blocks, blankets A small number of seated and lying poses with short pauses between, then rest.
Restorative Very slow, almost no movement 5 to 10 minutes or more Always, many: bolsters, blankets, blocks, sometimes a chair or wall Four to six fully supported shapes, with time to set up each one, then rest.
Iyengar Slow and precise, with demonstrations 30 seconds to several minutes Always, many: blocks, straps, blankets, chairs, wall A weekly theme, demonstration, practice and refinement of a few poses, rest. Beginners focus on standing poses.
Ashtanga Brisk and steady, a set rhythm About 5 breaths per pose Rarely The same set series every time: Sun Salutations, standing sequence, seated series, finishing sequence, rest.

A slow breath in class takes about 6 seconds, so 5 breaths is roughly 30 seconds. Studios may also heat the room for some of these styles; the schedule usually says so.

Hatha

haṭha

What a class looks like

On a modern schedule, "Hatha" usually signals a general, moderately paced class. You come into a pose, stay for 3 to 8 breaths, come out, and move on, often with a short pause in between to notice what changed. A typical hour opens with a few minutes of seated breathing or gentle warm-ups such as Cat-Cow, moves into standing poses like Tree Pose and Extended Triangle Pose, then comes down to the floor for seated poses, gentle backbends such as Cobra or Bridge, and twists. It closes with several minutes of rest lying on the back in Śavāsana. Blocks, a strap and a blanket are common but not always used, and music depends on the teacher.

A short history

In its historical sense, haṭha names a family of physical and breath practices described in medieval Sanskrit manuals such as the Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā, a fifteenth-century text. Today the word works mostly as an umbrella. Nearly every posture-based class is technically haṭha yoga, so studios use the label for classes that do not follow a more specific method.

Who might enjoy it

People who like time to settle into each pose, clear spoken instructions, and a mix of standing and floor work. If you want to learn pose names and shapes before you move quickly, a Hatha class gives you room to look around and copy.

Before your first class, ask

  • Is this class open to beginners, or does it assume you already know the poses?
  • How long are poses usually held, and is it fine to rest in between?
  • Are mats and props provided, or should I bring my own?

On this site

Our pose guides are written the Hatha way: one pose at a time, with entry steps, cues and a hold time. The 40-minute Gentle Full Practice follows the same arc as a general class, from seated breathing and warm-ups through Warrior II, Triangle and Tree to floor poses and a long rest.

Vinyasa

vinyāsa
Moderate to brisk 1 to 5 breaths per pose Props occasional

What a class looks like

  • Movement is linked to breath: one movement on the inhale, the next on the exhale.
  • Poses are held for 1 to 5 breaths, so the class rarely stops moving.
  • A common arc: warm-up, several rounds of Sun Salutations, a flowing standing sequence that builds toward a "peak pose," then floor work and rest.
  • The sequence changes from class to class and from teacher to teacher.
  • Blocks come out occasionally. Many teachers play music.

A short history

Vinyāsa is a Sanskrit word often translated as "placing in a special way." The breath-linked approach traces back to the teaching of T. Krishnamacharya in Mysore, India, in the 1930s, and was carried forward by students including K. Pattabhi Jois. Modern "flow" classes keep the link between breath and movement but drop the set sequence, so each teacher builds their own.

Who might enjoy it

People who like continuous movement, variety from one class to the next, and a rhythm set by the breath. If you enjoy dance, or simply prefer not to stay still for long, a beginner flow class is a good first step. If you like time to study each pose, you may want a slower style first and Vinyasa later.

Before your first class, ask

  • What level is this class, and will slower options be offered along the way?
  • Is the room heated?
  • Will the teacher call poses by English or Sanskrit names?

On this site

The 10-Minute Morning links Cat-Cow to the breath and moves through Downward-Facing Dog, a Low Lunge and Mountain Pose arm sweeps, the same building blocks used in a Sun Salutation. Learn Downward-Facing Dog and Cobra Pose well; you will meet both many times in a flow class. Breathwork Basics explains ujjāyī, the soft, audible breath many flow teachers cue.

Yin

What a class looks like

Yin is slow and almost entirely on the floor. A class might include only eight to twelve poses, each held for 3 to 5 minutes and sometimes longer. Rather than working the muscles to hold a shape, you settle into a modest version of it and stay. Bolsters, blocks and blankets set the height so you can remain without straining, and between poses there is often a short pause lying on the back. Yin uses its own names for many familiar shapes: Butterfly is a seated fold with the soles of the feet together, Sphinx is a low backbend on the forearms, and Dragon is a lunge.

A short history

Yin grew out of the long-held floor work of Paulie Zink, a martial arts and Taoist yoga teacher. It was developed and popularized from the late 1980s onward by Paul Grilley, and later by teachers including Sarah Powers. Teachers in this lineage often describe the long holds in terms of connective tissue, and they usually name a "target area," such as the hips or the spine, for each pose.

Who might enjoy it

People who like stillness, long quiet stretches of time and a short list of poses. If you are curious about staying with one sensation for several minutes without adjusting it, Yin offers plenty of that.

Before your first class, ask

  • How long are the holds, and is it fine to come out early?
  • Are bolsters and blankets provided?
  • Will most of the class be on the floor? This matters if getting down and up is hard for you.

On this site

The 20-minute Evening Wind-Down is floor-based and holds its supported shapes, such as Reclined Bound Angle and Legs Up the Wall, for up to two and a half minutes. Child's Pose, a shape you will meet in almost every Yin class, has a full guide with supported versions.

Restorative

The stillest style on this list: a handful of poses, each one built from props until nothing has to work to hold you there.

What a class looks like

A class may include only four to six poses, each held for 5 to 10 minutes or more, with the body fully supported by bolsters, folded blankets, blocks and sometimes a chair or the wall. Typical shapes include a supported reclining pose with the soles of the feet together (Supta Baddha Koṇāsana), legs resting up a wall (Viparīta Karaṇī) and Child's Pose draped over a bolster. A good part of the class goes into arranging props, and the room is usually quiet and dim.

A short history

Restorative yoga grew out of B.K.S. Iyengar's use of props to support students in poses they could not yet hold on their own. Judith Hanson Lasater, a longtime student of his, popularized the style in the United States through decades of teaching and writing.

Who might enjoy it

People who like a slow, quiet room, plenty of time to get comfortable and very little movement. It also suits anyone who enjoys the craft of building a supportive setup from blankets and bolsters.

Before your first class, ask

  • Are props provided, or should I bring my own?
  • Should I wear layers or socks? You will be still for long stretches and may feel cool.
  • Are any poses inverted, such as legs up the wall, and is there an alternative?

On this site

Our Props & Modifications guide shows how to choose and fold a bolster and a blanket. The Evening Wind-Down uses the same kind of supported floor shapes, and Practice at Home Setup covers space and gear.

Iyengar

What a class looks like

Iyengar classes are slow, precise and detailed. Poses are held for anywhere from 30 seconds to several minutes, and the teacher often stops the group to demonstrate a single action, such as how to turn the back thigh in Extended Triangle Pose, before everyone tries again. Props are central: blocks, straps, folded blankets, chairs, the wall and sometimes ropes attached to the wall. Beginner courses lean heavily on standing poses, and many teachers plan each week around a theme, such as forward bends or twists. Music is rare, and instructions are spoken plainly and often.

A short history

The method was developed by B.K.S. Iyengar (1918 to 2014), who taught in Pune, India, and who learned from T. Krishnamacharya. His 1966 book Light on Yoga brought the approach to an international audience. The method is known for its emphasis on alignment and for using props so that students can work toward the shape of a pose within their current range.

Who might enjoy it

People who like detail, clear structure, demonstrations and the chance to repeat one pose several times. If you enjoy knowing exactly where your feet, knees and hands go, this is a satisfying room.

Before your first class, ask

  • Is there an introductory course, and do I need to start there?
  • Has the teacher trained specifically in the Iyengar method?
  • How should I tell you about injuries or health conditions before class?

On this site

Our "Choose your version" sections use props in a similar spirit: every supported version is a complete pose. The Extended Triangle Pose guide and the Props & Modifications guide are good companions, and How to Read a Yoga Cue explains the kind of alignment language you will hear.

Ashtanga

aṣṭāṅga

The name means "eight limbs," a reference to the eight-part path described in the Yoga Sūtra attributed to Patañjali. On a schedule it means one specific method: the same set sequence, practiced in the same order, every time.

What a class looks like

Each pose is usually held for about five breaths, and short linking movements called vinyasas connect one pose to the next. Students breathe with ujjāyī and rest the gaze at set points (dṛṣṭi). Props are rare. Classes come in two forms: a led class, where the teacher counts the whole group through the sequence together, and Mysore-style, where each student works through the series at their own pace in a shared room while the teacher moves around giving individual instruction. Beginners learn the series a few poses at a time.

The order of a practice is always the same:

  1. Several rounds of Sun Salutations A and B (Sūrya Namaskāra), sometimes after a short opening chant.
  2. A set standing sequence, including Extended Triangle Pose.
  3. The series itself. For most students this is the Primary Series, made up largely of seated forward bends and hip rotations.
  4. A finishing sequence of backbends, inversions and seated poses.
  5. Rest, lying on the back.

A short history

Ashtanga Vinyasa is the method taught by K. Pattabhi Jois (1915 to 2009), who studied with T. Krishnamacharya and taught in Mysore, India, for decades. The method is organized into set series practiced in the same order, and traditional schedules ask students to practice most days of the week.

Who might enjoy it

People who like routine, a clear way to track their practice and a brisk, steady rhythm. If you enjoy memorizing a sequence and repeating it until it becomes familiar, Ashtanga gives you exactly that.

Before your first class, ask

  • Is this a led class or Mysore-style?
  • Is there a beginner or short-form class I should start with?
  • In a Mysore room, when should I arrive, and how long should a beginner stay?

On this site

Our sequences do not follow an Ashtanga series, but our guides cover shapes you will meet early on: Downward-Facing Dog, Extended Triangle Pose and Cobra Pose, a gentler shape some teachers offer in place of Upward-Facing Dog. Read the ujjāyī section of Breathwork Basics before your first led class.

Decision guide

How to choose

Choose by what you like about moving, not by what you think you should be able to do. Find the line that sounds most like you.

  • You want to learn the poses before moving quickly: start with Hatha, or a beginner Iyengar course.
  • You like to keep moving and enjoy rhythm: try a beginner Vinyasa class.
  • You want the same sequence every time: try a led Ashtanga class, then Mysore-style once the order is familiar.
  • You prefer stillness and long holds on the floor: try Yin.
  • You like being fully propped up, with very few poses: try Restorative.
  • You enjoy detail and demonstrations: try Iyengar.
  • Getting down to and up from the floor is hard: look for chair-based or gentle classes, ask whether an Iyengar class uses chairs, and ask before you book anything listed as floor-based.
  • You are not sure: book one class each in two styles that sound appealing, and compare how the pace and structure suited you.

Questions worth asking any studio

  • Is this class suitable for someone new to this style?
  • How long is the class, and how much of it is on the floor?
  • Are mats and props provided?
  • Can I rest or skip a pose at any point?
  • Is the room heated?
  • How do I let the teacher know about an injury or health condition?

Whatever you choose, it is always fine to rest in Child's Pose or sit out a pose. A good teacher expects it and will not single you out.

Practice with care

This page describes how classes are commonly run. It is general movement education, not medical advice. Check with a qualified healthcare professional before starting a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery or managing a health condition. Tell any teacher about injuries before class, stop if you feel pain, and work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. Read the Health & Safety Disclaimer.
