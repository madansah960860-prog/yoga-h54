How to Read a Yoga Cue: What Common Phrases Ask Your Body to Do

"Lengthen your spine." "Square your hips." "Find your edge." Teachers pack whole movements into a few words. Here are twelve of the most common cues, unpacked into what your joints and muscles are actually being asked to do.

By Rosa Delgado Published 10/12/2026 6 min read

Yoga cues are compressed instructions. "Lengthen your spine" or "square your hips" packs a whole movement into three words, and if nobody has unpacked it for you, it is easy to guess wrong. A cue is a suggestion about direction, not a rule about shape. Teachers use them because they cannot touch every student or see every angle at once.

Once you understand the action behind a cue, you can apply it to any version of a pose, including the chair and wall versions in our guides. Below, each cue is grouped by the part of the body it mostly concerns.

Cues about the spine

"Lengthen your spine"

This asks you to stop slumping by gently increasing the distance between your pelvis and the base of your skull. In practice, you lift through the crown of your head while your lower ribs stay soft instead of flaring forward. It does not mean flattening your back: your spine has natural curves, and the cue keeps them, just without the sag. In Downward-Facing Dog, bending your knees is often the quickest way to let the spine lengthen.

"Hinge at the hips"

This asks you to fold forward by bending at the hip joints, so your pelvis tips forward together with your spine, instead of rounding your back from the waist. Picture your pelvis as a bowl tipping its contents forward. In a standing forward fold, a slight bend in the knees usually makes a true hinge possible when the hamstrings are tight.

"Draw the navel in"

This is a gentle inward draw of the lower belly, a small fraction of your full effort, so the deep abdominal muscles wrap around your trunk like a wide belt. It is not sucking your stomach in or holding your breath. You should still be able to breathe freely into the sides and back of your ribs.

Cues about the hips, legs and feet

"Square your hips"

This asks you to turn your pelvis so both hip points (the bony knobs at the front of the pelvis) face the same direction, usually the front of the mat. In Tree Pose, it means not letting the standing hip jut out to the side.

It does not fit every pose. In wide-legged shapes such as Extended Triangle Pose, the pelvis is meant to open to the side, and forcing it square can twist the front knee. Many teachers now say "level your hips" instead. If squaring makes a knee twist or pinch, let your hips face where they comfortably face.

"Soften your knees"

This asks you to unlock your knees: stop pressing them back as far as they go, so the muscles around the joint share the work instead of the joint resting at its limit. You will see it in standing poses and forward folds. Softening is a slight bend you can feel, not a deep squat.

"Keep a micro-bend"

A micro-bend is the smallest possible version of a soft knee, often invisible from outside. It matters most for people whose knees naturally extend past straight (sometimes called hyperextension), because it keeps the leg muscles engaged instead of letting the knee drift backward. If your knees stop at straight, a micro-bend may make little difference.

"Ground through your feet"

This asks you to spread your weight across three points of each foot, the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe and the center of the heel, and press down evenly. You may notice the arch lifting slightly as you do. It gives every standing pose a stable base, and the same idea applies to your hands in Down Dog.

Cues about the shoulders and trunk

"Drop your shoulders"

This asks you to let your shoulder blades slide down your back, away from your ears, so your neck has room. With your arms overhead, the cue changes. In Downward-Facing Dog, our guide asks you to widen across the shoulder blades and turn the upper arms slightly outward, because forcing the shoulders down with the arms raised can create a pinch at the top of the shoulder.

"Engage your core"

This asks you to firm the muscles that wrap your trunk, including the deep abdominals, the muscles along the spine and the pelvic floor, so your torso holds its shape while your arms or legs move. Think firming, not clenching. In Bridge Pose, it means your lower ribs do not flare upward as your hips lift. If "engage" makes you grip your belly or hold your breath, dial it back.

Cues about effort, gaze and breath

"Find your edge"

This asks you to move into a pose until you feel clear sensation, then stop there or ease back slightly. Your edge is where the stretch or effort is noticeable but you can still breathe smoothly and hold steady. It is not the furthest point you can force your way to, and it moves from day to day.

"Rest your gaze" or "find your dṛṣṭi"

This asks you to choose one still point to look at, usually on the floor or wall in front of you. In balance poses like Tree Pose, a steady gaze gives your eyes a fixed reference while your feet make small corrections. Choose a point that is not moving, and keep your face soft.

"Breathe into your ribs" (or your hips, or your back)

Air only goes into your lungs, so you cannot literally breathe into your hip. These cues ask you to direct attention to an area while you breathe. With the ribs, they also describe something real: let the ribs widen to the sides and back as you inhale. In a forward fold, "breathe into your back" means noticing the back ribs expanding against the stretch. Read these as cues for attention, not anatomy.

When a cue does not fit your body

Cues are written for an average body that nobody actually has. Your arms may be long compared with your torso, your hip sockets may be shaped so that bone meets bone before a muscle feels any stretch, and an old injury may change what a joint can do. A cue that works for most people may not work for you, and that is information, not failure.

Three questions make the call easier:

  1. Can I still breathe smoothly? If following the cue makes you hold your breath, ease off.
  2. Do I feel it where the pose intends? A hamstring stretch belongs in the back of the thigh, not behind the knee.
  3. Is there pinching or sharpness in a joint? If so, back out of the cue or choose a supported version.

A cue describes a direction to move in, not a shape you have to reach.

If a cue still does not make sense in your body, choose the chair or wall version in the pose guide, or ask a qualified teacher to look at your shape in person. Someone who can see you can tell you whether a cue applies to your body, which no written guide can do.

Putting it together

Pick one pose this week and read its guide with this list beside you. In Downward-Facing Dog you will meet "lengthen," "press evenly through your hands" and bent knees within a few lines. Try each cue on its own for a breath or two and notice what changes. Once cues stop sounding like a foreign language, the Glossary covers the Sanskrit terms you will hear in class, and Your First Two Weeks on the Mat gives you a plan for putting them to use.
