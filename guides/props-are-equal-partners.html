Chair, Wall, Block, Strap: Why Props Are Equal Partners

A prop does not make a pose easier or lesser. It changes the geometry: where the floor is, how long your levers are, what holds your weight and what you can feel. Here is how each one works.

By Rosa Delgado Published 10/26/2026 7 min read

In many beginner classes, props are introduced as a fallback: grab a block if you cannot reach the floor. That framing makes them sound like training wheels, something to outgrow. A more accurate way to think about a prop is as a change to the geometry of a pose. The shape and its purpose stay the same; the prop adjusts the parts that do not fit your body today.

This guide covers what the four most common props do mechanically, where they appear in our pose guides, how teachers use them at every level, which sizes to look for and what to use if you would rather not buy anything.

What a prop actually changes

Almost every prop does one or more of four things:

  • Floor height. A block or a chair seat brings the floor up to meet your hand, so you can keep your spine long instead of rounding to reach down.
  • Lever length. A strap extends your arm, so a foot that is out of reach becomes reachable without pulling your back out of shape. Bending a knee shortens a lever in the same way.
  • Support. A block, blanket or bolster under part of your body takes some of its weight, so muscles that would otherwise be holding you up can let go.
  • Feedback. A wall behind you, or a block squeezed between your thighs, gives you something to press against, so you can feel where you are in space instead of guessing.

Seen this way, a prop keeps the important actions of a pose intact while adjusting the details. That is why our guides list supported versions under "Choose your version" rather than in a footnote.

The chair

A chair raises the floor to seat height, usually 17 to 19 inches, and gives you a stable base for seated and standing work. It makes the biggest difference for anyone who finds getting down to the floor, or back up again, difficult.

In our Downward-Facing Dog guide, Chair Dog places your hands on the seat of a chair pushed against a wall. Your hips still hinge, your arms still reach overhead and your back still lengthens; the angle is simply less steep, so your hamstrings and shoulders carry less. In Tree Pose, holding the back of a chair keeps your standing leg working while taking the worry out of balancing. The whole Desk Reset sequence happens in a chair.

Choose a sturdy chair without wheels, with a flat seat. On a smooth floor, set it on your mat or brace it against a wall so it cannot slide.

The wall

A wall is the prop you already own, and it does two jobs: support and feedback. A hand on the wall in a balance pose gives you a fixed point of contact. Standing with your back against it shows you where vertical actually is, which can feel surprisingly different from where you thought it was.

Half Dog at the wall, from the Down Dog guide, puts your palms on the wall at hip height and walks you back into an L shape. Your head stays level with your heart and your wrists carry no weight at all. In Extended Triangle Pose, standing with your back body near a wall gives you a reference for keeping the chest open instead of tipping forward.

The block

A standard yoga block measures 4 by 6 by 9 inches, which gives you three heights from one prop depending on which face rests on the floor. Foam blocks are light and soft. Cork blocks are heavier, firmer and less likely to tip. Wood is the firmest of all. For home practice, two matching blocks are more useful than one.

In Extended Triangle Pose, a block under your lower hand raises the floor so both sides of your waist can stay long. In Bridge Pose, a block placed under the sacrum (the flat bone at the base of the spine) turns an active backbend into a supported one you can rest in. In Child's Pose, a block under the forehead keeps the neck in line when your head does not reach the mat.

Blocks give feedback too. Squeezing one gently between your thighs in Bridge Pose tells you straight away whether your knees are drifting apart.

The strap

A yoga strap is a length of cotton or nylon webbing with a buckle or loop at one end. Straps commonly come in 6, 8 and 10 foot lengths. A 6-foot strap covers most uses for shorter people, 8 feet is a sensible middle choice, and 10 feet gives taller people room to loop it around both feet or around the upper arms.

The strap changes lever length. In a seated forward fold, looping it around the soles of your feet lets you hold on with straight arms while your spine stays long, instead of rounding to grab your toes. Lying on your back, a strap around one foot lets you raise that leg toward the ceiling while the other rests on the floor. In both cases the strap keeps your pelvis and spine where the pose wants them while the hamstrings lengthen at their own pace.

How teachers use props at every level

Props are not only for beginners. Experienced teachers reach for them constantly, for reasons that have nothing to do with ability:

  • To isolate one action. A block between the thighs makes it easier to feel the inner legs working without thinking about everything else at once.
  • To stay longer. Supported versions let you remain in a shape for minutes instead of breaths, which is the basis of restorative and yin classes.
  • To see more clearly. A wall behind the back in a standing pose shows exactly where the body drifts.
  • To meet the day. The same teacher may practice Down Dog on blocks on a stiff morning and on the floor the next day.

A prop does not lower the standard of a pose. It changes the geometry so the standard can be met.

Household substitutes

You can practice everything on this site without buying a single prop. These stand-ins work well:

  • Block: a sturdy hardcover book or two, a firm cushion, or a stack of folded towels. Check that it will not slide or collapse under your weight.
  • Strap: a bathrobe belt, a long scarf or a necktie. Avoid anything elastic, which stretches just when you want it to hold steady.
  • Bolster: two bed pillows stacked and wrapped in a blanket, or a firm sofa cushion.
  • Blanket: any thick blanket or large bath towel, folded into a rectangle about 2 inches thick for kneeling or sitting.
  • Chair: a dining or kitchen chair without wheels. Skip folding chairs that can collapse and office chairs that roll.

Choosing your version

When you open a pose guide, read "Choose your version" first and pick the option that lets you keep the main actions of the pose: a long spine in Down Dog, level hips in Tree Pose, both sides of the waist long in Triangle. If that option uses a chair, a wall or two blocks, that is the pose for you today. For a pose-by-pose list of prop options, see Props & Modifications.

!

Props change the load, not the need for care. A supported version does not make every pose suitable for everyone. If you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery or managing a health condition, check with a qualified healthcare professional before you begin, and stop if anything hurts.
