Yoga Glossary

Plain-English meanings for the Sanskrit pose names, breath terms, alignment cues, anatomy words and props you will meet in our guides and in most classes. Every Sanskrit entry shows its spelling with diacritics and what the word literally means.

Search by any spelling, with or without accent marks, or filter by kind of term. Six entries link straight to a full pose guide.

Pronunciation primer

How to pronounce IAST

Sanskrit is usually written in Devanāgarī script. IAST, the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration, writes it in the Roman alphabet, adding small marks above and below letters for sounds English spelling cannot show. You do not need perfect pronunciation to practice; the marks simply help you recognize a word and say it with care.

ā ī ū
A line over a vowel means hold it about twice as long. ā sounds like the a in "father", ī like the ee in "see", ū like the oo in "food". A plain a is short, like the u in "but", so āsana sounds roughly like "AH-suh-nuh".
ṛ
A vowel, not a consonant: a lightly tapped r with a short i sound, close to the "ri" in "rich". Vṛkṣa (tree) sounds roughly like "vrik-shuh".
ś
"sh" as in "shine", made with the tongue just behind the teeth. Śavāsana begins with this sound.
ṣ
Also "sh", but with the tongue tip curled back toward the roof of the mouth. English speakers hear it as an ordinary "sh", which is close enough to be understood, as in dṛṣṭi.
ṭ ḍ ṇ
A dot below t, d or n means the tongue tip curls back to touch the roof of the mouth, giving a slightly hollow sound. Examples: Utkaṭāsana, Tāḍāsana, Trikoṇāsana.
ṅ
"ng" as in "sing". It appears before k or g, as in Bhujaṅgāsana, roughly "bhu-jung-gah-suh-nuh".
ñ
"ny" as in "canyon". It appears before c or j, as in Añjaneyāsana.
ṃ
A dot below m (called anusvāra) carries the vowel before it into a hum, somewhere between m and ng. Oṃ is a long o that closes into that hum.
c and h
The letter c is always "ch" as in "chair", so Candra is "chun-druh". An h after a consonant is a puff of breath: the th in Utthita is a breathy t, never the th in "think".
e and o
Always long: e like the ay in "say", o like the o in "go". Setu sounds like "say-too".

The search box below ignores these marks, so typing vrksasana finds Vṛkṣāsana. We also list the common spellings you may see elsewhere, such as drishti and shavasana.

Kind of term

A

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana (Downward-Facing Dog)
Literally "downward-facing dog pose" (adho, downward; mukha, face; śvāna, dog). An upside-down V with hands and feet on the floor and hips lifted, often used as a transition in flowing classes. Read the Downward-Facing Dog guide.
Ahiṃsā
Literally "non-harming" (a, not; hiṃsā, harm). The first of the ethical guidelines (yama) described in the Yoga Sūtra attributed to Patañjali. In a physical class, teachers often bring it up as a reminder not to force a pose.
Alignment
How the parts of your body are arranged in a pose, relative to each other and to the floor, for example the front knee over the ankle in a lunge. Alignment cues describe a useful starting point. Bodies differ, so your version may not match a photograph exactly.
Añjaneyāsana (Low Lunge)
Named for Añjaneya, a name of Hanumān meaning "son of Añjanā". A lunge with the back knee resting on the floor and the front knee bent over the ankle while the hips ease forward. A folded blanket under the back knee is common.
Apānāsana (Knees to Chest)
Named for apāna, one of the five currents of breath (vāyu) in yoga texts, associated with downward and outward movement. You lie on your back and hug both knees toward your chest, often between other floor poses.
Ardha
Literally "half". Marks a partial or halfway version of a pose, as in Ardha Uttānāsana (Half Forward Fold), where you lift halfway up from a fold with a long spine.
Ardha Candrāsana (Half Moon)
Literally "half moon pose" (ardha, half; candra, moon). A standing balance on one leg with the torso and lifted leg roughly parallel to the floor and the lower hand on the floor or a block. An intermediate pose; standing with your back to a wall makes it steadier.
Āsana
Literally "seat", from a root meaning "to sit". The general word for a yoga pose and the ending of most pose names: Tāḍāsana is "mountain pose", Bālāsana is "child pose".

B

Baddha Koṇāsana (Bound Angle)
Literally "bound angle pose" (baddha, bound; koṇa, angle). Seated with the soles of the feet together and the knees falling out to the sides. Sitting on a folded blanket and resting each knee on a block are both common.
Bālāsana (Child's Pose)
Literally "child pose" (bāla, child or young one). Kneeling with your hips toward your heels and your torso folded forward over your thighs, forehead resting down. The go-to rest in most classes. Read the Child's Pose guide.
Bandha
Literally "lock" or "binding". In some traditions, a specific muscular engagement held with the breath, such as mūla bandha at the pelvic floor or jālandhara bandha with the chin drawn toward the chest. In pose names such as Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana it means "building" or "construction".
Bhujaṅgāsana (Cobra Pose)
Literally "serpent pose" (bhujaṅga, serpent). A low backbend from the belly with the hands under the shoulders and the chest lifting forward and up. Read the Cobra Pose guide.
Blanket
A firm, foldable blanket, usually wool or cotton. Folded, it lifts the hips in seated poses, pads the knees in kneeling poses and supports the head in resting poses. A thick bath towel is a fine stand-in.
Block
A firm rectangle of cork, foam or wood, usually about 4 by 6 by 9 inches, so it can stand at three heights. It brings the floor closer to your hands or supports your hips and head. A stack of hardcover books works at home.
Bolster
A long, firm cushion, round or rectangular, used to support the body in resting and reclining poses. Two firm bed pillows stacked lengthwise can stand in for one.

C

Chair
A sturdy chair without wheels, ideally braced against a wall. It can bring the floor up to your hands, steady you in balance poses or let you practice seated. Our chair versions are complete poses in their own right. See Props & Modifications.
Core
The muscles that wrap the torso: the abdominals and obliques at the front and sides, the deep transversus abdominis, the back muscles, the diaphragm above and the pelvic floor below. "Engage your core" usually asks for a gentle, even firming around the waist, not sucking the belly in.
Counterpose
A pose that moves the spine or a joint in roughly the opposite direction to the pose before it, such as Child's Pose (a rounded spine) after Cobra (an arched spine). A common sequencing habit rather than a rule.
Cue
A short instruction from a teacher, such as "soften your knees" or "lengthen through the crown of your head". Many cues are metaphors; our guide How to Read a Yoga Cue translates common ones into plain movement.

D

Daṇḍāsana (Staff Pose)
Literally "staff pose" (daṇḍa, staff or stick). Sitting upright with your legs extended straight in front of you and your hands beside your hips. A folded blanket under the sitting bones lets the pelvis tip forward so the spine can stay tall.
Dhyāna
Literally "meditation" or "contemplation". The seventh of the eight limbs of yoga described in the Yoga Sūtra: sustained, steady attention. In a class it may appear as a few minutes of seated stillness at the end.
Diaphragm
The dome-shaped muscle beneath your lungs that does most of the work of breathing. As you inhale it flattens and moves down, which is why your belly and lower ribs widen; as you exhale it rises again.
Dṛṣṭi (often spelled drishti)
Literally "sight" or "gaze". A steady point to rest your eyes on during a pose, such as the floor a little ahead of you in Cobra or a still spot on the wall in Tree Pose. A fixed gaze gives balance poses a stable reference point.

E

Eka
Literally "one". Appears in names of poses that single out one limb, as in Eka Pāda Rājakapotāsana (One-Legged King Pigeon), where pāda means "foot" or "leg".
Engage
To switch a muscle on enough to hold a joint steady, without gripping. "Engage your thighs" in a standing pose means firm the front of the thighs so the kneecaps lift slightly. You should still be able to breathe easily.
Extension
A joint movement that opens the angle between two body parts, the opposite of flexion. Spinal extension is a backbend, as in Cobra and Bridge; hip extension moves the thigh behind you, as in the back leg of a lunge.

F

Flexion
A joint movement that closes the angle between two body parts. Spinal flexion rounds the back, as in Child's Pose; hip flexion brings the thigh and torso closer together, as in a forward fold.
Flow
An informal name for classes that link poses with the breath in a continuous sequence. See Vinyāsa.

G

Garuḍāsana (Eagle Pose)
Named for Garuḍa, the great bird of Hindu and Buddhist mythology, usually translated as "eagle". A standing balance with one leg wrapped over the other and the arms crossed and wrapped in front of the chest.
Gaze point
The English term for dṛṣṭi: a still point for your eyes during a pose. See Dṛṣṭi.
Gomukhāsana (Cow Face Pose)
Literally "cow face pose" (go, cow; mukha, face). A seated pose with the knees stacked and the hands reaching to clasp behind the back, one from above and one from below. A strap between the hands is common.
Grounding
A cue to press evenly into whatever touches the floor, such as all four corners of each foot in a standing pose. It sets a stable base for the rest of the shape.

H

Hamstrings
The three muscles along the back of each thigh, running from the sitting bones to just below the knee. They bend the knee and extend the hip. Forward folds and Downward-Facing Dog lengthen them; Bridge asks them to work.
Haṭha
Literally "force" or "effort". Historically, the branch of yoga built around physical practices such as poses and breathwork. Today a class labeled "hatha" usually means a slower-paced class that holds each pose for several breaths. See Styles Explained.
Hinge
To bend forward at the hip joints while the spine stays long, like a door swinging on its hinges. Most forward folds start with a hinge, and bending the knees lets you hinge farther without rounding the back.
Hip flexors
The muscles at the front of the hip that draw the thigh toward the torso, chiefly the iliopsoas and the rectus femoris. Lunges and Bridge lengthen them.
Hip points
The two bony points at the front of your pelvis (anterior superior iliac spines) that you feel when you rest your hands on your hips. "Point your hip points forward" means face your pelvis toward the front of the mat.

I

Inversion
Any pose that brings your head below your heart. Downward-Facing Dog and Standing Forward Fold are mild inversions. Headstand and shoulderstand are strong ones and are not taught on this site.
Iyengar
A style named after its founder, B.K.S. Iyengar (1918 to 2014), known for precise alignment, longer holds and generous use of props such as blocks, straps, chairs and walls. Many props found in classes today were popularized through this style. See Styles Explained.

J

Jānu Śīrṣāsana (Head-to-Knee Pose)
Literally "knee head pose" (jānu, knee; śīrṣa, head). A seated forward bend over one extended leg, with the other knee bent and its sole against the inner thigh. A strap around the extended foot keeps the spine long.

K

Koṇa
Literally "angle". Appears in Trikoṇāsana (three-angle, or triangle, pose), Baddha Koṇāsana (bound angle) and Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana (extended side angle).
Kumbhaka
From kumbha, "pot" or "jar", a vessel that holds. A pause after an inhale or an exhale in breathwork. The beginner patterns in Breathwork Basics use only short, optional pauses; leave them out if they feel strained.

L

Lengthen
A cue to create space by reaching two ends of the body away from each other, such as the crown of the head and the tailbone. It asks for length, not for a deeper stretch.
Locking
Pushing a joint, usually the knee or elbow, all the way to the end of its range so the muscles around it switch off. Teachers say "don't lock your knees" to keep those muscles working. See Micro-bend.

M

Malāsana (Garland Pose)
From mālā, "garland". A deep squat with the feet wide, heels down or resting on a rolled blanket, and the palms together in front of the chest. A block under the hips turns it into a supported seat.
Mantra
Often explained as "instrument of the mind" (man, to think; tra, tool). A word, sound or phrase repeated aloud or silently, sometimes chanted at the start or end of a class. Joining in is always optional, and listening quietly is just as welcome.
Mārjaryāsana-Bitilāsana (Cat-Cow)
Literally "cat pose" and "cow pose" (mārjāra, cat; bitilā, cow). On hands and knees, the spine alternates between rounding (Cat) on an exhale and arching (Cow) on an inhale. A seated version in a chair moves the spine the same way.
Mat
A thin, non-slip surface that keeps hands and feet from sliding and marks out your practice space. Rubber, cork and TPE are common materials; a non-slip rug on a firm floor works for many poses.
Micro-bend
A very slight bend in a straight joint, usually the knee, just enough that it is not locked. The leg still looks straight while the muscles around the knee stay active.
Mudrā
Literally "seal" or "gesture". A deliberate position of the hands, such as añjali mudrā, palms pressed together at the chest. These gestures carry symbolic meaning in several traditions; in most classes they are simple, optional hand positions.

N

Nāḍī śodhana (Alternate-Nostril Breathing)
Literally "channel clearing" (nāḍī, channel; śodhana, clearing). You breathe in through one nostril and out through the other, switching sides with a light touch of the fingers. Taught step by step in Breathwork Basics.
Namaste
Literally "a bow to you" (namas, bow or salutation; te, to you). A respectful greeting, often said with palms together at the end of a class. Saying it back is a courtesy, never a requirement.
Navāsana (Boat Pose)
Literally "boat pose" (nāva, boat). Balancing on the sitting bones with the torso leaning back and the legs lifted, shins parallel to the floor or legs straight. Your hands can hold the backs of your thighs, or your feet can stay on the floor.
Neutral spine
The spine's natural resting shape, with its gentle curves at the neck, upper back and low back intact: neither rounded forward nor arched back. Many poses, such as Tabletop and Mountain Pose, start from here.

O

Oṃ
Also written om or aum. A syllable regarded as sacred in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, sometimes chanted at the beginning or end of a class. You may join in, hum along or simply listen; all three are respectful.

P

Pārśvottānāsana (Pyramid Pose)
Literally "intense side stretch pose" (pārśva, side or flank; uttāna, intense stretch). A standing forward fold over the front leg with both legs straight and the hips facing forward. Blocks under the hands keep the spine long.
Paścimottānāsana (Seated Forward Bend)
Literally "intense stretch of the west" (paścima, west, meaning the back of the body, which faces west when you practice toward the sunrise; uttāna, intense stretch). Seated with the legs extended, folding forward from the hips. A strap around the feet and a blanket under the sitting bones are common.
Phalakāsana (Plank Pose)
Literally "plank pose" (phalaka, plank or board). The top of a push-up, with the body in one straight line from head to heels. Lowering the knees, or pressing the hands into a wall or a kitchen counter, keeps the same shape with less load.
Prāṇa
Often translated as "breath" or "life force". In yoga texts it names the animating force associated with breathing and being alive; in everyday class language it often simply means the breath.
Prāṇāyāma
Literally "extension of breath" (prāṇa, breath; āyāma, extension or regulation). The general name for breathing practices that shape the length and pattern of the breath. Start with Breathwork Basics.
Props
Any object that supports a pose: blocks, straps, blankets, bolsters, a chair or a wall. Props change where the floor is, so a pose can meet your body as it is today. See Props & Modifications.

Q

Quadriceps
The four muscles along the front of each thigh that straighten the knee. They work in standing poses such as Triangle and Chair Pose. Bridge and lunges lengthen the one that also crosses the hip, the rectus femoris.

R

Restorative
A style of practice that uses bolsters, blankets and blocks to support the body fully in a small number of long, still holds, often several minutes each. The aim is comfortable stillness rather than effort. See Styles Explained.

S

Śalabhāsana (Locust Pose)
Literally "locust pose" (śalabha, locust or grasshopper). A backbend from the belly in which the chest and legs lift together with the arms reaching back. Lifting only the chest, or one leg at a time, is a common beginner version.
Sama vṛtti (Equal Breathing)
Literally "equal movement" (sama, equal; vṛtti, movement or fluctuation). Breathing with inhales and exhales of the same count, such as 4 in and 4 out. The breath pacer in Breathwork Basics can keep the count for you.
Śavāsana (Final Rest)
Literally "corpse pose" (śava, corpse). Lying still on your back at the end of practice, usually for several minutes. A blanket over you, a bolster under your knees or resting on your side are all complete versions.
Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana (Bridge Pose)
Literally "bridge-building whole-body pose" (setu, bridge; bandha, building; sarva, all; aṅga, limb). Lying on your back with your feet planted and your hips lifted. Read the Bridge Pose guide.
Sitting bones
The two rounded bones at the base of the pelvis (ischial tuberosities) that you sit on. "Lift your sitting bones" in Downward-Facing Dog means tilt your pelvis so they point up and back.
Square the hips
A cue to turn both hip points so they face the same direction, usually the front of the mat, as in a lunge or Pyramid Pose. Not every pose asks for it: in Triangle and Warrior II the hips stay open to the side.
Stack
A cue to line one joint up directly over another, such as shoulders over wrists in Tabletop or knee over ankle in a lunge, so the bones carry weight in a straight line.
Strap
A non-stretch fabric strap, usually 6 to 10 feet long, with a buckle. It extends your reach when your hands cannot meet or reach your feet. A belt or a long scarf works at home.
Sukhāsana (Easy Seat)
Literally "easy" or "pleasant" pose (sukha, ease or comfort). A simple cross-legged seat. Sitting on a folded blanket so your knees can drop below your hips, or sitting on a chair, keeps it easy in practice as well as in name.
Supta
Literally "asleep" or "reclined". Marks a pose done lying on your back, as in Supta Baddha Koṇāsana (Reclining Bound Angle) and Supta Pādāṅguṣṭhāsana (Reclining Hand-to-Big-Toe).
Sūrya Namaskāra (Sun Salutation)
Literally "salutation to the sun" (sūrya, sun; namaskāra, salutation). A set series of poses linked with the breath, often used to begin a class. Versions range from gentle chair sequences to quicker flowing ones.

T

Tāḍāsana (Mountain Pose)
Literally "mountain pose" (tāḍa, mountain, though some read it as "palm tree"). Standing tall with your feet hip-width apart and your arms by your sides. The starting point and reference shape for most standing poses.
Tailbone
The small, tapered bone at the very base of the spine (coccyx). "Lengthen your tailbone toward your heels" usually means let the back of the pelvis drop slightly so the low back does not overarch.

U

Ujjāyī
Literally "victorious". Breathing through the nose with a slight narrowing at the back of the throat, which makes a soft, steady sound often compared to distant waves. Common in flowing classes. See Breathwork Basics.
Ūrdhva
Literally "upward" or "raised". Appears in Ūrdhva Hastāsana (Upward Salute, arms reaching overhead) and Ūrdhva Mukha Śvānāsana (Upward-Facing Dog).
Ūrdhva Mukha Śvānāsana (Upward-Facing Dog)
Literally "upward-facing dog pose". A backbend from the belly with straight arms and the thighs lifted off the floor, weight on the hands and the tops of the feet. A step beyond Cobra; many classes offer Cobra in its place.
Utkaṭāsana (Chair Pose)
Literally "powerful pose" (utkaṭa, powerful or fierce). Standing with the knees bent and the hips reaching back as if to sit, arms forward or overhead. Hovering just above the seat of a real chair is a full version.
Uttānāsana (Standing Forward Fold)
Literally "intense stretch pose" (ut, intense; tān, to stretch). A standing fold from the hips with the head hanging. Bent knees and hands on blocks or a chair seat keep the spine long.
Utthita Trikoṇāsana (Extended Triangle Pose)
Literally "extended three-angle pose" (utthita, extended; tri, three; koṇa, angle). A wide-legged standing pose reaching over the front leg with the chest turned to the side. Read the Extended Triangle Pose guide.

V

Vinyāsa
Literally "placing in a special way" (vi, in a special way; nyāsa, placing). Moving from one pose to the next in time with the breath, and the name for flowing class styles built on that idea. See Styles Explained.
Viparīta Karaṇī (Legs Up the Wall)
Literally "inverted action" (viparīta, reversed or inverted; karaṇī, doing). Lying on your back with your legs resting up a wall, often with a folded blanket under the hips. A mild inversion.
Vīrabhadrāsana I and II (Warrior I and II)
Named for Vīrabhadra, a fierce warrior figure in Hindu mythology. Two lunging standing poses: in Warrior I the hips and chest face forward with the arms overhead; in Warrior II they open to the side with the arms reaching front and back.
Vṛkṣāsana (Tree Pose)
Literally "tree pose" (vṛkṣa, tree). Balancing on one leg with the other foot resting on the standing ankle, calf or inner thigh, never on the knee. Read the Tree Pose guide.

W

Wall
A clear stretch of wall is the steadiest prop in your home. It supports you in balance poses, takes weight off the wrists and gives your body a fixed reference for "straight" and "to the side".

Y

Yin
A style of long, quiet floor holds, often 3 to 5 minutes each, with the muscles kept as passive as is comfortable. The name comes from yin and yang in Chinese philosophy, not from Sanskrit. See Styles Explained.
Yoga
From the Sanskrit root yuj, "to yoke" or "to join". A family of practices and philosophies with a long history in South Asia, of which physical poses are one part. On this site we teach poses, breath and simple, steady attention.
