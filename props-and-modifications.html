Props & modifications guide

A block, a strap or a chair changes where the floor is, how far you have to reach and how much of your weight you carry. This guide covers seven props: what each one does mechanically, how to choose it, where to use it in our pose guides, and what to grab from around the house instead.

At the end you will find five modification patterns that explain almost every supported version you will ever see, so you can adapt poses we have not written about yet.

Every version is the pose

A supported version is not a lesser version. A pose is a shape and a relationship between body parts, not a measure of how close your hands get to the floor.

  • Tree Pose with a hand on the wall still asks the standing leg to balance and the hips to stay level. The wall takes away the worry of falling, so you can pay attention to those things.
  • Extended Triangle Pose with the lower hand on a block keeps both sides of the waist long, which is what the pose is about. Reaching the floor by collapsing the top shoulder misses it.
  • Downward-Facing Dog with the hands on a chair seat keeps the same long spine and overhead arms while carrying less weight through the wrists.
  • Choosing a prop is a decision about today's body, not a grade. Many experienced students use blocks and straps every day, and some poses are rarely practiced without them.

That is why every one of our pose guides lists its supported versions under "Choose your version," with equal space and equal detail. Browse the Pose Library

The mat

What it changes

A mat does two jobs. It grips, so your hands and feet stay put when you press into them, and it adds a thin layer of cushion between bony points and the floor. Grip matters most in standing poses and in Downward-Facing Dog, where hands and feet push away from each other. Cushion matters most for knees, wrists and the spine in floor work. More cushion is not always an advantage: on a thick, soft mat the standing foot sinks and wobbles in balance poses.

How to choose

  • Size: a standard mat is about 24 inches wide and 68 to 72 inches long. If you are taller than about 6 ft, look for 74 inches or more so your head and heels both fit when you lie down.
  • Thickness: 1/8 inch (about 3 mm) gives the firmest base for balance; 3/16 inch is a middle ground; 1/4 inch (about 6 mm) adds cushion for knees and floor work.
  • Surface: natural rubber grips well but is heavy, smells at first and is not suitable if you have a latex allergy. PVC and TPE mats are lighter. Cork-topped mats feel dry under damp hands.
  • Test: press a palm into the mat and push forward. The mat should not slide, and the surface should not bunch.

Try it in

  • Downward-Facing Dog: spread the fingers wide and press the knuckles into the mat so the hands do not creep forward.
  • Tree Pose: a thin mat gives the standing foot a firm base. If yours is thick, try the pose on the bare floor beside it.
  • Child's Pose: fold the end of the mat over once to double the padding under the knees.
  • Cobra Pose: the mat cushions the front of the hips and the tops of the feet.

Household substitute

A low-pile rug on a non-slip pad, or a dry wood floor for standing poses with a folded blanket for kneeling and lying down. Avoid loose throw rugs on smooth floors; they slide.

Blocks

What they change

A block raises the floor. When your hand cannot reach the ground without rounding the spine or twisting the shoulders, a block meets it partway. Under the body, a block takes weight: slid under the sacrum in Bridge Pose, it holds the hips up so the legs and back can rest. Every block offers three heights, depending on which face you turn down.

How to choose

  • Size: a standard block measures about 4 by 6 by 9 inches, giving heights of 4, 6 or 9 inches. Thinner 3-inch blocks are lighter but less steady on their edge.
  • Cork: dense, heavy (around 2 lb) and grippy. It stays put on the floor and under weight, which makes it the steadier choice for sitting on or leaning into.
  • Foam: light (well under 1 lb) and softer against bony points, but it can tip or squash under weight. Comfortable under the head or hands.
  • Wood or bamboo: very firm and steady, and hard against bony points.
  • Buy two. Many poses use one under each hand.

Try them in

  • Extended Triangle Pose: lower hand on a block outside the front shin, at whatever height keeps both sides of the waist long.
  • Bridge Pose: lift the hips, slide a block under the sacrum (the flat bone at the base of the spine), and rest your weight on it.
  • Downward-Facing Dog: hands on two blocks set shoulder-width apart, braced against a wall if they slide.
  • Child's Pose: rest the forehead on a block when it does not reach the mat.

Household substitute

Two or three hardcover books stacked and tied with a belt, or a sturdy shoebox packed with paper. For the forehead in Child's Pose, stacked fists work too.

Which height?

  • 9 inches (standing on end): under the hand in Triangle when the hamstrings feel tight, or under the hands in Down Dog for the most lift.
  • 6 inches (on its side): the usual height under the sacrum in Bridge, and a steady middle ground for the hands.
  • 4 inches (lying flat): under the forehead in Child's Pose, or as a low, wide seat.

The strap

6 to 10 ft long Cotton webbing D-ring or cinch buckle

What it changes

A strap lengthens your arms. When your hands cannot meet behind your back or reach your feet without rounding, the strap bridges the gap so the spine stays long and the shoulders stay broad. Buckled into a loop, it can also hold two body parts at a set distance, such as the thighs in Bridge Pose, so your muscles do not have to do that job.

How to choose

  • Length: 6 ft suits shorter people and most arm work; 8 ft covers nearly every use; 10 ft is handy if you are tall or want to loop it around both legs.
  • Material: cotton webbing about 1.5 inches wide grips well and does not dig in.
  • Buckle: a metal D-ring (two rings) adjusts quickly and holds firmly; a plastic cinch buckle is lighter and also works.

Try it in

  • Bridge Pose: loop the strap around the thighs just above the knees, at hip width, so the knees stay in line with the hips.
  • Downward-Facing Dog: loop it around the upper arms just above the elbows, at shoulder width, so the elbows do not splay outward.
  • Reclined Leg Stretch (Supta Pādāṅguṣṭhāsana): hold the strap around the sole of the lifted foot instead of reaching for the toes. Find it in the Pose Library.
  • Cow Face arms (Gomukhāsana): hold the strap between your hands when they do not meet behind your back.

Household substitute

A belt, a bathrobe tie, a long scarf, or a dish towel for shorter reaches. Avoid anything stretchy, such as an exercise band, which pulls back against you.

Bolsters and blankets

The soft props. Where a block raises the floor, these two take your weight and fill the gaps between you and the ground, which is why they appear in almost every slow, floor-based practice.

The bolster

A bolster is a firm, dense cushion that carries your weight for long periods. Instead of your muscles holding a shape, the bolster does. Laid lengthwise under the spine, it lifts the rib cage so the chest widens; laid under the knees, it lets the legs rest bent.

How to choose

  • Shape: a rectangular bolster (about 24 to 28 inches long, 12 inches wide and 6 inches high) sits flat and does not roll. A round bolster, about 9 inches across, gives a higher, curved support.
  • Fill: cotton batting or a firm fiber fill holds its shape. Buckwheat hulls mold to you but are heavy.
  • Cover: a removable, washable cotton cover with a zip, and a carrying handle.

Try it in

  • Child's Pose: knees wide, the bolster lengthwise between them, torso and one cheek resting on it.
  • Bridge Pose, supported: rest the pelvis on a bolster laid crosswise, with the shoulders and head on the floor.
  • Reclined Bound Angle (Supta Baddha Koṇāsana): lie back along a bolster with the soles of the feet together and a block under each knee.
  • Final rest (Śavāsana): a bolster under the knees lets the legs rest slightly bent.

Household substitute

Two firm bed pillows stacked inside one pillowcase, a firm sofa cushion, or two blankets folded, rolled together and tied with a strap.

The blanket

A folded blanket adds a little height and a lot of padding. Under the hips in a seated pose, a few inches of lift lets the pelvis tip forward so the spine can stack upright. Under the knees or wrists, it softens contact with the floor. Rolled, it fills small gaps, such as under the ankles or the neck.

How to choose

  • Material: flat-woven wool or cotton that holds a crisp fold. Fleece and plush throws slide and flatten.
  • Size: roughly 50 by 70 inches or larger, so it folds into a firm rectangle about 3 inches thick.
  • Quantity: two if you can, one to sit or kneel on and one to cover yourself during the final rest.

Try it in

  • Child's Pose: a rolled blanket under the ankles if the tops of the feet feel pressed, or folded between the hips and heels.
  • Cobra Pose: a thin fold under the front of the hips pads the hip bones against the floor.
  • Bridge Pose: a folded blanket under the shoulders, with the head on the floor off the blanket, adds room under the back of the neck.
  • Easy Seat (Sukhāsana): sit on the edge of a folded blanket so the knees are level with or lower than the hips.

Household substitute

A large bath towel or beach towel, folded several times. A towel is thinner than a blanket, so fold it more.

The chair

A chair brings the floor up to seat height, or replaces the floor altogether. Seated, it lets you practice the upper half of a standing pose without bearing weight on the legs. Standing, its back gives your hand a steady rail for balance, and its seat offers a high, stable surface for the hands in folds and in Downward-Facing Dog. For anyone who finds getting down to and up from the floor hard, a chair keeps the whole practice within reach.

How to choose

  • Sturdy, with four legs: no wheels, no swivel and no rocking.
  • Armless, so your arms and legs can move out to the sides.
  • A flat seat about 17 to 19 inches high, so your feet rest flat with the knees near hip height.
  • A folding chair works if it locks open. Set it on a mat or push its back against a wall so it cannot slide.

Try it in

  • Downward-Facing Dog: hands on the seat with the chair against a wall, then step back until your arms and back make one long line.
  • Tree Pose: hold the chair back with one hand, or sit and rest one foot against the opposite ankle with the knee turned out.
  • Extended Triangle Pose: lower hand on the chair seat instead of the shin or the floor.
  • Cobra Pose, seated: sit toward the front of the seat, hold its sides behind your hips, and lift the chest forward and up.
  • Child's Pose, seated: feet wide, fold forward over a pillow on your lap, or rest your forearms on a table.

Household substitute

A sturdy kitchen chair or a heavy bench without casters. For standing balance, a kitchen counter works as well as a chair back.

The wall

The wall is the one prop every home already has. It gives you a steady vertical reference: something to press into, lean on or line up against. Pressing your hands into a wall lets you work the shape of an inversion or backbend while staying upright. Resting your back body against it shows you exactly where your hips and shoulders are in space, which is hard to sense on your own.

How to choose

  • A clear stretch of wall about 3 ft wide, with nothing hanging that you could knock with a hand or a heel.
  • Place the short end of your mat against the baseboard so the mat cannot slide away from the wall.
  • Skip doors, which can swing open, and stretches of wall with radiators or vents at floor level.

Try it in

  • Downward-Facing Dog: Half Dog at the wall, palms at hip height, walking back into an L shape.
  • Tree Pose: stand side-on with a hand on the wall, or with your back lightly against it.
  • Extended Triangle Pose: let the back body lightly touch the wall so both shoulders and the back of the pelvis stay in one plane.
  • Cobra Pose, standing: hands on the wall at shoulder height, then lift the chest forward and up while the hips stay over the feet.

Household substitute

A kitchen counter or the back of a heavy sofa can stand in for the wall in standing poses. Avoid bookcases and anything else that could tip.

Modification patterns

Almost every supported version in our guides is built from one of five patterns. Once you can name them, you can adapt poses we have not written about yet, and you will recognize what a teacher is offering when they suggest a change in class.

Five modification patterns, what each one changes, and examples
Pattern What it changes Examples
Raise the floor Brings the ground up to meet you, so you do not have to round or twist to reach it. Block under the lower hand in Extended Triangle; hands on a chair seat in Down Dog; blanket under the hips in Easy Seat.
Shorten the lever Brings an arm or leg closer to the joint it moves from, so the muscles hold less load. Bent knees in Down Dog; hands at the chest rather than overhead in Tree Pose; knees down in Plank (Phalakāsana).
Add support Lets a prop carry part of your body weight, so muscles can let go. Bolster under the torso in Child's Pose; block under the sacrum in Bridge; back body against the wall in Triangle.
Reduce range Goes a shorter distance into the shape while keeping its direction. Low Cobra with the elbows bent; kickstand foot in Tree Pose; hips lifted only a few inches in Bridge.
Change orientation Keeps the relationship between body parts but changes how gravity acts on them. Half Dog at the wall instead of Down Dog; seated Tree on a chair; Cobra standing at the wall.

Patterns combine

Half Dog at the wall changes orientation and raises the floor at the same time. Bridge on a block adds support and shortens the range. If a pose still does not feel right after one change, add a second pattern rather than pushing harder into the first.

How to tell a version fits you today

  • You can breathe smoothly through the nose.
  • You feel the shape the pose describes, such as length along the back of the legs, without sharp or pinching sensations.
  • You could stay for the suggested hold without gripping.

Practice with care

This guide is general movement education, not medical advice. Check that every prop is steady before you put weight on it, and check with a qualified healthcare professional before starting a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery or managing a health condition. Stop if you feel pain, and work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance with props. Read the Health & Safety Disclaimer.
