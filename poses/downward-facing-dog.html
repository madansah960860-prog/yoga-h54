Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

An upside-down V that lengthens the whole back of the body, from the hands through the shoulders and spine to the hamstrings and calves. It shows up in almost every flowing class, and it has a version for nearly every body.

Beginner Mild inversion Blocks, wall or chair optional
Level
Beginner
Suggested hold
5 breaths, about 30 seconds
Body area
Hamstrings, calves, shoulders, spine
Props
Optional: 2 blocks, wall or chair

What happens in your body

Downward-Facing Dog is a hip hinge with your arms overhead and your weight shared between hands and feet. Here is what lengthens, what works, what moves and how the load is shared.

Lengthens

  • Hamstrings along the back of the thighs
  • Calves (gastrocnemius and soleus)
  • Latissimus dorsi along the sides of the back

Works

  • Shoulder muscles holding the arms overhead
  • Serratus anterior, which keeps the shoulder blades wide
  • Quadriceps, gently, as the legs straighten

Joints in motion

  • Hips flex deeply
  • Shoulders flex overhead
  • Wrists extend and bear weight
  • Ankles bend as heels reach down

Sharing the load

  • Arms and legs share the work
  • Bent knees shift effort into the legs
  • Knuckles pressing keep load off the wrist crease
  • Shaky arms mean knees down and rest

Pose breakdown

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana
Beginner Hold 30 to 60 seconds

Entry steps

  1. Come onto hands and knees in Tabletop, wrists under shoulders and knees under hips.
  2. Walk your hands forward about one hand-length. Spread your fingers wide with the index fingers pointing forward.
  3. Tuck your toes under. On an exhale, lift your knees and send your hips up and back.
  4. Keep your knees generously bent at first so your spine can lengthen.
  5. Straighten your legs only as far as your back stays long. Bent knees are a complete version of this pose.
  6. Let your head hang between your upper arms, ears roughly in line with your arms.
  7. To come out, lower your knees to the mat on an exhale and sit back into Child's Pose.

Alignment cues

  • Press evenly through all ten knuckles, especially the base of the index finger and thumb, so the load spreads across the whole hand.
  • Turn your upper arms slightly outward so the shoulders widen away from your ears and your neck has room.
  • Lift your sitting bones toward the ceiling. A long spine matters more than straight legs.
  • Let the heels reach toward the floor. They do not need to touch it, and for many people they never will.
  • Keep feet about hip-width apart with the outer edges parallel, like two rails of a track.
  • Draw your lower ribs gently in so your chest does not sag toward the floor.
  • Soften your neck and jaw, and let your gaze rest between your feet or on your knees.

Breath pattern

  • Breathe slowly in and out through the nose.
  • On each inhale, lengthen from your hands to your hips.
  • On each exhale, let your heels sink a little and your chest drift toward your thighs.
  • If your breath becomes short or strained, lower your knees and rest.
  • Count breaths rather than seconds. It keeps your attention on the pose instead of the clock.

Hold duration

5 breaths about 30 seconds

  • New to the pose: 3 to 5 breaths, then rest in Child's Pose.
  • With regular practice: build toward 1 minute.
  • In a flowing sequence: often a single breath between other poses.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding the back to force the legs straight. Bend your knees until the spine can lengthen.
  • Shoulders hunched up by the ears with the head pushed toward the floor. Turn the upper arms out and let the head hang.
  • Weight collapsing into the heels of the hands, which loads the wrists. Press through the knuckles and fingertips.
  • A stance so short that the pose turns into a rounded fold. Step your feet back a few inches.
  • A stance so long that the hips drop toward a sagging Plank. Walk your feet in until the hips can lift.
  • Locking the knees hard once the legs straighten. Keep a slight bend behind each knee.

Progression

  • Three-Legged Dog: on an exhale, lift one leg in line with your hips, keeping both hip points level. Hold 3 breaths and switch.
  • Longer holds: once 5 breaths feel steady, add one breath per week up to 1 minute.
  • Pedaling the feet: bend one knee, then the other, to explore the calves before settling.
  • Plank to Dog: shift forward into Plank on an inhale and press back to Dog on an exhale, 3 to 5 rounds. Keep your knees down in Plank if you like.

Contraindications and care

  • Avoid or use the wall or chair version if you have a wrist, elbow or shoulder injury, or carpal tunnel symptoms.
  • Keep your head above your heart (wall or chair version) if you have high or low blood pressure, glaucoma, recent eye surgery, or are prone to dizziness or vertigo.
  • During pregnancy, especially later on, ask your healthcare provider first and favor the wall or chair version.
  • After recent surgery or a back injury, wait for clearance from your healthcare provider.
  • Stop if you feel pain, numbness or tingling. Work with a qualified teacher in person for hands-on guidance on your alignment.

Choose your version

Each of these is Downward-Facing Dog. They keep the same long spine and overhead arms while changing how much weight your wrists, shoulders and hamstrings carry.

Wall

Half Dog at the wall

  • Stand facing a wall and place your palms on it at hip height, shoulder-width apart.
  • Walk your feet back until your torso is level with the floor and your body makes an L shape.
  • Bend your knees as much as you need to keep the back long.
  • Keeps your head level with your heart and takes weight off the wrists.
Chair

Chair Dog

  • Push a sturdy chair against a wall so it cannot slide.
  • Place your hands on the seat (or the top of the backrest for less depth).
  • Step back and hinge at the hips until your arms and back form one long line.
  • Useful when getting down to the floor is not practical.
Blocks

Hands on blocks

  • Set two blocks shoulder-width apart at the front of your mat, on any height.
  • Place your hands on the blocks before lifting your hips.
  • Raises the floor so the hamstrings and shoulders ask less of you.
  • Brace blocks against a wall if they slide on your floor.
Knees

Bent-knee Dog

  • Keep both knees well bent for the whole hold.
  • Press your belly toward your thighs and lift the sitting bones.
  • Lets the spine lengthen fully when hamstrings are tight.
  • Many experienced students practice it this way every day.

Where this pose shows up

Downward-Facing Dog appears in two of our guided sequences, and it pairs naturally with the poses below.

10-Minute Morning

Beginner, 10 minutes. Down Dog comes third, for 45 seconds, and leads into a Low Lunge on each side.

Gentle Full Practice

Beginner, 40 minutes. Down Dog comes fourth, straight after Child's Pose, for 60 seconds.

Practice with care

This page is general movement education, not medical advice. Talk with a qualified healthcare professional before starting a new practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery or managing a health condition. If your wrists, elbows or shoulders object, the wall and chair versions above keep the same shape without the load on your hands, and they keep your head above your heart.
