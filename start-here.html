Start Here: A Beginner's Guide to Yoga at Home

Everything worth knowing before your first practice: what you will actually do, what to have nearby, how our guides are laid out, and a plan for your first seven days.

There is no test to pass and nothing to buy. If you can spare ten minutes and a patch of floor, or a sturdy chair, you have what you need to begin.

What a yoga practice actually involves

Set aside the magazine covers. A home practice is three things happening together: you move your body into and out of shapes, you breathe in a steady, deliberate way, and you pay attention to what you notice. None of the three requires a particular body, age or fitness level.

Movement

  • Standing shapes that ask your legs to hold you up
  • Folds, gentle backbends, side bends and twists that move your spine in each direction
  • Balances, where small muscles in your feet and ankles make constant adjustments
  • Resting shapes on the floor or in a chair between efforts

Breath

  • Breathing in and out through the nose when that is comfortable
  • Pairing a movement with an inhale or an exhale
  • Counting breaths in a pose instead of watching a clock
  • Keeping the breath moving during effort, never holding it to push through

Attention

  • Noticing where you feel a stretch or effort
  • Telling strong sensation apart from pain
  • Staying in one pose for a few breaths without rushing ahead
  • Coming back when your mind wanders, without scolding yourself

A typical beginner session at home lasts 5 to 20 minutes. You might start seated or on your hands and knees, move through a handful of standing poses, spend a minute balancing, and finish lying down or sitting quietly. That arc, a gentle start, some effort in the middle and a rest at the end, runs through every sequence on this site.

You will also meet Sanskrit names, the traditional names for poses, printed in italics beside the English. Downward-Facing Dog, for example, is Adho Mukha Śvānāsana. You never need to learn them, but they are handy when a teacher in a class uses them.

What you need, and what you do not

You can practice everything on this site with what is already in most homes. A mat is handy, and a few household objects stand in for props until you decide whether to buy any.

Useful to have

  • A clear patch of floor about 7 feet long and 4 feet wide, so you can lie down with your arms overhead and reach out to each side.
  • A yoga mat, or a rug that does not slide on your floor. Bare feet give you the most grip.
  • Clothes you can bend, lunge and lie down in without adjusting them.
  • A sturdy chair without wheels and a stretch of bare wall. Many poses use them, and the Desk Reset sequence happens entirely in a chair.
  • A blanket or two, plus two firm pillows or hardcover books if you do not own yoga blocks. A belt or long scarf works as a strap.

Not required

  • Flexibility. You do not need to touch your toes, sit cross-legged or kneel comfortably to begin.
  • Special clothing, an expensive mat or any particular brand of equipment.
  • An hour a day. Short, regular sessions are a sensible way to learn the shapes.
  • Experience, or a class on day one, though a teacher can be valuable later.

Our Practice at Home Setup guide covers floor surfaces, lighting and storage. Props & Modifications explains what each prop changes in a pose and what to use in its place.

How to read our pose guides

Every pose guide follows the same order, so once you have read one you know where to find everything in the next. Here is what you will meet, from the top of the page down.

  1. The overview. The English name, the Sanskrit name in italics, a short description and a strip of key facts: level, suggested hold, body areas involved and any props.
  2. What happens in your body. Three short lists covering what lengthens, what works and which joints move. This is the mechanics of the shape, not a promise about what it will do for you.
  3. The pose breakdown. One card that always runs in the same sequence: entry steps, alignment cues, breath pattern, hold duration, common mistakes, progression, and finally contraindications and care.
  4. Choose your version. Two to four ways into the same pose, such as at the wall, with a chair, on blocks or with bent knees. Each one is presented as a complete version, because it is.
  5. Where this pose shows up. The sequences that include it and the poses that pair well with it, so you know where to go next.

Difficulty levels

Beginner

Shapes most people can try in their first weeks, each with at least one supported version that keeps the floor, a chair or a wall close by.

Intermediate

Shapes that ask for more strength, balance or range of motion. Try them once the related beginner poses feel familiar.

Advanced

Demanding shapes such as deep backbends and arm balances. We describe them, but in-person teaching is the sensible way to learn them.

A level describes the pose, not the person. Plenty of experienced students choose the chair version of a beginner pose on a given day, and that is still the full practice.

Day 1 to Day 7

Your first week, day by day

This plan uses three of our guided sequences and a few poses from the library. Sessions run 5 to 20 minutes. Follow the order or swap days around; what matters is showing up for a few minutes, not completing a checklist.

  1. Day 1: Set up and meet Child's Pose Bālāsana

    Clear your practice space and lay out your mat. Read the Child's Pose guide, then try two versions: forehead on the mat, and forehead resting on a block or stacked fists. Finish with two minutes of slow breathing through your nose in a comfortable seat.

    5 min

  2. Day 2: The 10-Minute Morning

    Follow the 10-Minute Morning with its on-page timer. Use the chair and wall swaps listed under the sequence for any pose that feels unfamiliar, and pause the timer whenever you like.

    10 min

  3. Day 3: Desk Reset

    Practice the Desk Reset in a sturdy chair without wheels. It shows how much of a practice fits in a seat, and it is easy to repeat on a workday.

    8 min

  4. Day 4: Two pose guides

    Read the Downward-Facing Dog and Cobra Pose guides. Try each pose in two of its versions under "Choose your version," holding 3 to 5 breaths, and rest in Child's Pose between them.

    10 min

  5. Day 5: Evening Wind-Down

    Try the Evening Wind-Down, a slower, floor-based practice. Have two blocks or firm pillows and a blanket nearby; a bolster and a strap are optional.

    20 min

  6. Day 6: Morning again, plus Tree Pose Vṛkṣāsana

    Repeat the 10-Minute Morning and notice what feels more familiar than it did on Day 2. Afterward, read the Tree Pose guide and practice it beside a wall for 5 breaths on each side.

    15 min

  7. Day 7: Rest or review

    Take the day off, or spend a few minutes with Bridge Pose and Child's Pose. Then jot down two notes: one pose that felt good, and one question you would like to ask a teacher.

    0 to 10 min

Have nearby this week

  • A mat or a rug that does not slide
  • A sturdy chair without wheels
  • A clear stretch of wall
  • Two blocks, firm pillows or hardcover books
  • A blanket for under your knees or hips

Missed a day? Pick up where you left off. There is nothing to catch up on.

When the week is done, Your First Two Weeks on the Mat carries the plan into a second week, and the 40-minute Gentle Full Practice is there whenever you want a longer session.

Safety first

Yoga is physical activity, and the same common sense applies as with any new exercise. These points appear on every pose and sequence page; here they are in one place.

Before you begin

Talk with a qualified healthcare professional before starting a new physical practice, especially if you:

  • are pregnant or have recently given birth;
  • have a recent injury, or ongoing joint, back or neck concerns;
  • are recovering from surgery;
  • are managing a health condition such as high or low blood pressure, glaucoma, osteoporosis or a heart condition;
  • take medication that affects your balance or makes you dizzy.

Our guides cannot see you or know your history. A professional who can is the right person to tell you what to avoid.

Sensation or warning sign?

A stretch can feel strong without being harmful. Pain is different. Use this as a rough guide, and when in doubt, come out.

Sensations that are usually fine compared with signs to stop
Usually fine to stay withStop and come out
A broad, even stretch along a muscle, such as the back of the thighSharp, stabbing or pinching pain, especially in or near a joint
Muscles working hard enough to tremble a littleNumbness, tingling or pain that travels down an arm or leg
Effort that fades within moments of leaving the poseDizziness, chest discomfort or breathlessness that does not settle with rest
Mild muscle soreness a day or two after something newPain that lingers after you stop, or grows with each attempt
!

Stop with pain

If something hurts, come out of the pose slowly, rest, and either try a supported version another day or leave that pose out. Never push through pain to reach a shape in a photograph. Our guides are general movement education, not medical advice.

Choosing a style, and learning to breathe

Styles

Class names can be confusing. Hatha, vinyasa, yin and restorative classes use many of the same poses but differ in pace, in how long poses are held and in how many props come out. Slower styles with longer holds give you time to read cues and find your version. Faster flowing styles link each movement to a single breath and assume you already know the shapes.

Our sequences are slow and steady, closest to a gentle hatha class. Styles Explained compares the common styles side by side, so a class description makes sense before you sign up.

Breath

Most instructions in our guides pair a movement with a breath: lift on the inhale, fold on the exhale. To begin, breathe through your nose when it is comfortable, keep the breath moving during effort, and let each exhale be as long as the inhale, or a little longer.

Counted patterns and formal breathing practices, known as prāṇāyāma, can come later. Breathwork Basics walks through simple counted breathing with an on-screen pacer you can follow.

When to find a teacher

A page can explain a pose. It cannot watch you do it. A qualified teacher in the room can see where you are working hard, where you are sinking into a joint and which version suits you today.

  • Consider a teacher early if you are managing an injury or health condition, if a pose consistently causes discomfort, or if you simply want feedback.
  • Look for beginner or gentle classes with a small group, and a teacher who offers props and alternatives without being asked.
  • Ask any teacher about their training and experience. Backgrounds vary widely, and a good teacher will be glad to tell you.
  • Before class, mention anything relevant, such as a recent injury, a pregnancy or a pose you have been told to avoid.

What this site is, and what it is not

Before you rely on anything here, it is worth knowing exactly what you are reading.

What it is

  • General movement education: plain-language descriptions of how poses and breathing exercises are done and what moves in your body.
  • Free to use. Every pose guide and sequence, the hold timer and the breath pacer are available without an account.
  • Written by our founder and lead teacher, Rosa Delgado, who completed a 200-hour and a 300-hour yoga teacher training.
  • Built around equal versions: chair, wall and prop options are presented as complete poses.

What it is not

  • Medical advice, a diagnosis or a care plan of any kind.
  • A clinical service. We are yoga teachers, not doctors, clinicians or other licensed healthcare providers, and nothing here is tailored to your health history.
  • A promise of results. We describe what you may notice in a pose, not what it will do for your health.
  • A substitute for a qualified teacher who can see you in person.
