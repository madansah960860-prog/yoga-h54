Breathwork basics

Six simple breathing techniques you will meet in yoga classes, each explained step by step with counts, practice times, common mistakes and who should skip or modify it. An on-screen pacer lets you count along.

Nothing here asks you to hold your breath or push your lungs anywhere uncomfortable. The aim is to understand how breathing works and to practice a few patterns with clear instructions, at a pace that feels easy on the day.

What "breathwork" means in a yoga class

In a yoga class, breathwork usually goes by the Sanskrit name prāṇāyāma. Teachers use it in two ways. Sometimes it is a short, separate practice: a few minutes of counted or shaped breathing at the start or end of class while you sit still. More often it is woven into the movement. You are asked to inhale as the arms lift and exhale as you fold, so the breath sets the tempo of the class.

Prāṇāyāma is often translated as extending or regulating the breath. Older yoga texts describe many techniques, some involving long breath holds and strong, rapid breathing. Most general classes today use a small, gentle subset: noticing the breath, slowing it, evening out the inhale and exhale, and changing its sound or its route through the nose. Those are the techniques on this page.

Think of breathwork as a skill, like learning to balance in Tree Pose. You practice it, it becomes familiar, and you adjust it to your own body on the day. There is no target to hit and no prize for the longest count.

Words you may hear in class

  • prāṇāyāma: breath practices in general.
  • sama vṛtti: "equal movement," an inhale and exhale of the same length.
  • ujjāyī: breathing through the nose with a slight narrowing at the back of the throat, which makes a soft sound.
  • nāḍī śodhana: alternate-nostril breathing, closing one nostril at a time with the fingers.
  • kumbhaka: a deliberate pause with the lungs full or empty. Optional, and never required on this page.

More terms are in our Glossary, and Styles Explained shows which classes lean on the breath most.

How breathing works, physically

You do not need anatomy to breathe, but a clear picture of the moving parts makes the instructions in class much easier to follow. Here is what moves when you breathe quietly at rest.

Diaphragm

  • A dome-shaped sheet of muscle under the lungs, attached around the lower ribs, the base of the breastbone and the front of the lumbar spine.
  • On the inhale it contracts and flattens downward, making room for the lungs to fill.
  • As it descends it nudges the abdominal contents down and forward, which is why the belly rises.
  • On a quiet exhale it lets go and domes back up with very little effort.

Ribs

  • Small muscles between the ribs, the intercostals, lift and widen the rib cage on the inhale.
  • The lower ribs swing out to the sides, like the handle of a bucket lifting.
  • The upper ribs and breastbone move forward and up, more like a pump handle.
  • The back ribs move too. Lie down and you can feel them press into the floor.

Nose and mouth

  • The nose warms, moistens and filters the air on its way in.
  • The nasal passages are narrower than the mouth, so nose breathing tends to be slower and quieter.
  • The mouth moves more air with less resistance, which is why it opens when you work hard.
  • Most yoga classes ask you to breathe through the nose unless told otherwise.

When breathing takes effort

Breathe harder and more muscles join in. The abdominal muscles tighten to push air out faster, and muscles in the neck and upper chest lift the top of the rib cage. That is normal during effort. In quiet breathing practice, though, you will usually be invited to let the neck and shoulders stay soft and let the diaphragm and ribs do the work.

If your nose is blocked

Breathe gently through the mouth, or through a mix of nose and mouth. None of the techniques below needs to be forced through a blocked nose. Alternate-nostril breathing is simply one to skip that day.

Setting up: seat, posture and timing

Any of these three positions works for every technique on this page. Choose the one that lets you stay comfortably still for five minutes; each is a complete way to practice.

Floor

On the floor

  • Sit on a folded blanket or firm cushion so your hips are a little higher than your knees.
  • Cross the legs loosely, or kneel with a block between the shins.
  • Rest your back against a wall if sitting upright tires you.
Chair

On a chair

  • Use a sturdy chair without wheels.
  • Sit toward the front edge with your feet flat and hip-width apart.
  • If your feet do not reach the floor, rest them on a block or a thick book.
Lying

Lying down

  • Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat, or with a bolster under your knees.
  • Put a folded blanket under your head if your chin tips up.
  • Later in pregnancy, or whenever lying flat is uncomfortable, lie on your side or sit instead.

Posture checklist

  1. Let the sitting bones settle evenly, so you are not leaning to one side.
  2. Stack the rib cage over the pelvis, without lifting the chest or slumping.
  3. Let the shoulders rest, with the hands on the thighs, palms up or down.
  4. Draw the chin slightly in so the back of the neck is long.
  5. Soften the jaw, lips together and teeth slightly apart.
  6. Close your eyes, or rest your gaze on the floor a few feet ahead.

Timing

Short, regular sessions are easier to keep up than long, occasional ones, and 3 to 5 minutes is plenty to begin. Any time of day works. Practice on a comfortable stomach, ideally an hour or two after a large meal, and keep a clock or the pacer in view so you are not guessing how long you have been sitting.

Step by step

Six techniques, starting with attention

Begin with the first two, which ask nothing of the breath except attention and a soft belly. Add the others one at a time, over days or weeks. Each card lists the steps, counts, how long to practice, common mistakes, and who should skip or modify it.

1. Breath Awareness

The foundation for everything else: you watch the breath without changing it.

Steps

  1. Sit or lie in one of the setups above and let your hands rest.
  2. Close your eyes or lower your gaze.
  3. Notice where you feel the breath most clearly: the nostrils, the chest, the ribs or the belly.
  4. Count each full breath, in and out, from 1 to 10, then start again at 1.
  5. If you lose count, begin again at 1. Losing count is part of the practice.

Counts

No set count. Let each breath be whatever length it is; you are counting breaths, not seconds.

How long

Start with 3 rounds of 10 breaths, about 2 to 3 minutes. Build to 5 minutes if you enjoy it.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to make each breath deeper or slower than it wants to be.
  • Lifting the shoulders or holding the belly in.
  • Judging the breath as "wrong." There is no wrong breath here.

Skip or modify

Almost anyone can try this. Keep your eyes open if closing them feels unsteady or unpleasant. If attention on the breath itself is uncomfortable, rest your attention on the feeling of your hands on your thighs instead. Stop if you feel lightheaded.

2. Diaphragmatic (belly) Breathing

Here you invite the diaphragm to lead, so the belly moves more than the upper chest.

Steps

  1. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat, or sit tall in a chair.
  2. Rest one hand on your belly just below the ribs and the other on your upper chest.
  3. Breathe in through the nose and let the belly rise into your lower hand. The upper hand stays fairly still.
  4. Breathe out through the nose and let the belly fall back without pushing.
  5. After a few breaths, let the hands rest and keep the same easy pattern.

Counts

In for about 4, out for about 4 or 5. Any comfortable count works; the movement matters more than the number.

How long

Begin with 5 to 10 breaths. Build to 3 to 5 minutes over a couple of weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Pushing the belly out with force instead of letting it rise.
  • Arching the low back off the floor on each inhale.
  • Taking in so much air that you feel lightheaded.
  • Clenching the jaw or shoulders to keep the chest still.

Skip or modify

Later in pregnancy, practice seated or lying on your side rather than flat on your back. After abdominal surgery or with a hernia, ask your healthcare provider first. If lying down is uncomfortable, sit. Stop if you feel lightheaded or short of breath.

Counting the breath

The next two techniques give the breath a length. Both have a matching pattern on the breath pacer further down the page, and both work just as well with a silent count in your head.

3. Equal Breathing

sama vṛtti
Beginner In 4, out 4 Pacer: Equal 4 / 4

Steps

  1. Sit tall, or lie down with knees bent.
  2. Breathe naturally for a few rounds and notice roughly how long your inhale and exhale are.
  3. Breathe in through the nose for a count of 4.
  4. Breathe out through the nose for a count of 4.
  5. Keep the two counts the same and the breath smooth, with no hold at either end.

Counts

4 / 4 in, out

  • Choose 3 / 3 if 4 / 4 feels long.
  • Move to 5 / 5 only if 4 / 4 stays easy for several minutes.

How long

  • 1 to 3 minutes to begin.
  • Build toward 5 minutes over a few weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a count that is too long, then gasping to catch up.
  • Counting faster on the exhale than on the inhale.
  • Making the breath loud and effortful to fill the count.

Skip or modify

Some teachers add equal holds after the inhale and the exhale, which makes a "box" pattern. The holds are optional. Leave them out if you are pregnant, prone to dizziness, or managing blood pressure, a heart condition or a lung condition such as asthma. Stop and breathe normally if you feel lightheaded.

4. Extended Exhale

The exhale is made a little longer than the inhale, and the breath stays smooth and quiet throughout.

Steps

  1. Sit or lie comfortably.
  2. Breathe in through the nose for a count of 4.
  3. Breathe out slowly through the nose, or through softly pursed lips, for a count of 6.
  4. Let a natural pause arrive at the bottom of the exhale if it wants to. Do not hold it.
  5. Use the "Longer exhale 4 / 6" pattern on the pacer if a visual count suits you.

Counts

4 / 6 in, out

  • Try 3 in, 5 out if 4 / 6 feels long.
  • Move toward 4 in, 8 out only if it stays effortless.

How long

  • 6 to 10 rounds to begin, about 1 to 2 minutes.
  • Build to 5 minutes as it becomes familiar.

Common mistakes

  • Squeezing out the last of the air with the belly and ribs.
  • Shortening the inhale to make the exhale feel longer.
  • Hunching forward as the exhale goes on.

Skip or modify

If you feel short of breath or lightheaded, return to equal counts or to natural breathing. With asthma or another lung condition, keep the counts short and ask your healthcare provider about breathing exercises before you begin. During pregnancy, keep every count comfortable and never strain.

Shaping the breath

The last two techniques change the breath's sound or its route. Learn them after the first four feel familiar, and keep them light.

5. Ujjāyī Breathing

ujjāyī

Breathing through the nose with a slight narrowing at the back of the throat, which gives the breath a soft, steady sound. You will hear it in many Vinyasa and Ashtanga classes.

Steps

  1. Sit tall. Open your mouth and breathe out with a soft "haaa," as if fogging a mirror.
  2. Notice the gentle narrowing at the back of your throat that makes the sound.
  3. Repeat the same exhale with your mouth closed, breathing out through the nose. The sound continues, quieter.
  4. When that feels familiar, keep the same light narrowing on the inhale.
  5. Aim for a sound that only you, or someone right beside you, could hear.

Counts

No set count. Many people settle at 4 to 6 counts each way. In a flowing class the breath sets the pace and the movement follows it.

How long

1 to 3 minutes, seated, while you learn it. In a flow class it may continue throughout, but you can drop back to plain nose breathing at any time.

Common mistakes

  • Squeezing the throat so hard that the breath rasps or catches.
  • Tightening the jaw, tongue or face.
  • Making the sound loud enough to fill the room.
  • Holding the breath at the top or the bottom.

Skip or modify

Return to plain nose breathing if you feel lightheaded, strained or short of breath, or if your throat is sore. During pregnancy, keep the sound light and never strain. If you are managing blood pressure or a heart condition, skip any version a teacher pairs with breath holds.

6. Alternate-Nostril Breathing

nāḍī śodhana

You breathe through one nostril at a time, closing the other gently with the fingers of one hand.

Steps

  1. Sit tall and rest your left hand in your lap.
  2. Fold the index and middle fingers of your right hand toward the palm, leaving the thumb and ring finger free.
  3. Close the right nostril with your thumb and breathe in through the left.
  4. Close the left nostril with your ring finger, open the right, and breathe out through the right.
  5. Breathe in through the right, close it, open the left, and breathe out through the left. That is one round.
  6. Switch sides after every inhale. Finish by breathing out through the left, then lower the hand.

Counts

In 4, out 4, with no holds. Traditional versions add breath holds; save those for in-person instruction with a qualified teacher.

How long

5 rounds to begin, about 1 to 2 minutes. Build to 5 minutes over a few weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Pressing so hard that the nose is pushed sideways. A light touch is enough.
  • Letting the elbow sag until the arm tires. Rest it on a cushion or on your other hand.
  • Losing track of the side. Remember: switch after each inhale.
  • Tipping the chin up or the head to one side.

Skip or modify

Skip it when your nose is blocked. Practice without holds if you are pregnant, prone to dizziness, or managing blood pressure or a heart condition. If raising the arm is uncomfortable, support the elbow, or simply picture the route of the breath without using the hand. Stop if you feel lightheaded.

Alternate-nostril breathing: the thumb closes one nostril while the ring finger waits to close the other.

Count along

Breath pacer

The pacer is a visual counting aid. The arch fills while "Breathe in" shows and empties while "Breathe out" shows, and the number counts down the seconds in each phase. It does not measure or record anything; it simply keeps time.

Breathe comfortably rather than forcing the count. If a pattern feels long, switch to Gentle 3 / 3, or stop following the numbers and simply watch the arch while you breathe at your own pace. No holds are required.

  1. Choose a pattern. Equal 4 / 4 is selected to start.
  2. Settle into your seat and let a couple of natural breaths go by.
  3. Press Start pacing, and breathe in when "Breathe in" appears.
  4. Follow along for 1 to 3 minutes, then press Stop and sit for a few natural breaths.

The four patterns

  • Equal 4 / 4: in 4, out 4. Matches Equal Breathing.
  • Longer exhale 4 / 6: in 4, out 6. Matches Extended Exhale.
  • Box 4 / 4 / 4 / 4: in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4. The pacer shows "Hold" during the pauses, and they are optional: keep breathing slowly through them if you prefer. Skip this pattern if you are pregnant, prone to dizziness, or managing blood pressure or a heart condition.
  • Gentle 3 / 3: in 3, out 3. A shorter count for any day.

If your device is set to reduce motion, the arch will not animate, but the words and numbers still change on time.

Ready

Pattern

A five-minute starter routine

Once the first few techniques feel familiar, string them together. This routine adds up to exactly five minutes, and you can do it seated, on a chair or lying down.

  1. Breath Awareness

    Count 10 natural breaths, then another 10 if there is time.

    1:00

  2. Diaphragmatic Breathing

    One hand on the belly, one on the chest. Let the belly lead.

    2:00

  3. Equal Breathing sama vṛtti

    Use the pacer on Equal 4 / 4, or Gentle 3 / 3 if that suits you better.

    1:30

  4. Natural breathing, eyes open

    Let go of all counting and look around the room before you get up.

    0:30

When that feels easy, swap step 3 for Extended Exhale, or add two minutes of ujjāyī. Breath practice also sits naturally at the start of a sequence: the 10-Minute Morning opens with a minute of slow breathing in Easy Seat, and the Evening Wind-Down begins with two.

Breathe within your comfort

These techniques are general movement education, not medical advice. Stop and return to normal breathing if you feel lightheaded, dizzy, tingly or short of breath. Avoid breath holds if you are pregnant, prone to dizziness, or managing blood pressure, a heart condition or a lung condition, and check with a qualified healthcare professional before you begin if any of these apply to you.

Never practice breathing exercises while driving, in water, or anywhere a moment of dizziness could be unsafe. For hands-on guidance, work with a qualified teacher in person. Read the Health & Safety Disclaimer.
