Practice at home setup

You do not need a dedicated room. You need a patch of clear floor, a surface that keeps your hands and feet from slipping, a wall and a sturdy chair. This page walks through each one, then shows how to fit practice into an ordinary week.

Everything here works in a living room, a bedroom or a corner of a kitchen. Most of it costs nothing, and every prop has a household stand-in.

Measure your space Printable checklist

How much space you need

A yoga mat is about 2 ft wide and 6 ft long. Around it, you want roughly 3 ft of clear floor on every side, so that a sideways step, an arm reaching out in Extended Triangle Pose or a leg stepping back into a lunge does not meet a table edge. That adds up to a footprint of about 8 by 12 ft. If your room is smaller, keep the clear space at the sides and the back of the mat, where arms and legs travel most.

Ceiling height

Stand in the middle of your mat and reach both arms straight overhead. Your fingertips should clear the ceiling, any hanging lights and any ceiling fan with a few inches to spare. A standard 8-foot ceiling works for most adults; sloped attic ceilings, low beams and ceiling fans are the usual trouble spots. Turn a ceiling fan off before you start, even if it seems high enough.

Measure it in three minutes

  1. Lay your mat down where you plan to practice.
  2. Stand at the middle of the mat and reach both arms out to the sides. Nothing should be within fingertip reach.
  3. From the front of the mat, step one foot far back into a long lunge. Your back foot should land on clear floor.
  4. Lie down and sweep your arms overhead along the floor. Check for furniture legs, cords and doors that open inward.

Flooring: what works and what slips

A firm, level floor makes balance and standing poses steadier, and a non-slip surface on top of it matters more than how soft it feels. Here is how common floors behave under a mat.

How common floor types behave under a yoga mat, with a setup tip for each
Floor What to expect Setup tip
Wood, laminate or vinyl Firm and level, the easiest base for standing and balance poses. Slippery under socks or damp feet. Practice barefoot on a mat. Sweep first so grit does not press through.
Tile or stone Firm, but hard and often cold underfoot, and unforgiving on the knees. Use a 1/4 inch mat, or fold a blanket under your knees for kneeling poses.
Low-pile carpet Comfortable and generally steady with a mat on top. Choose a thinner, grippy mat; a thick mat on carpet can feel spongy.
Thick or plush carpet Soft but unsteady. The mat can shift, and feet sink in balance poses. Use a thin, firm mat and keep a wall or chair within reach for balance poses.
Area rug on a hard floor Can slide across the floor beneath it, taking the mat with it. Put a non-slip pad under the rug, or roll the rug aside and use the mat on the bare floor.

A two-minute slip check

  • Press both palms into the mat and push forward, as if starting Downward-Facing Dog. The mat should not move.
  • Stand on one foot on the mat and shift your weight around. The mat should not shift on the floor.
  • Check the corners. A mat that curls up at the ends can catch a toe, so roll it the other way for a minute or set a block on each corner.
  • Look for cords, slippers, toys and pet bowls within 3 ft of the mat.

Bare feet grip a mat far better than socks. If your feet get cold, wear socks until you start moving and take them off for standing poses.

Temperature, light and sound

Temperature

Ordinary room temperature is enough. You will warm up as you move and cool down when you lie still, so keep a sweater or blanket nearby for the final rest. If you use a space heater, keep it well away from the mat and its cord out of your path.

Light

Any light you can see your mat and hands by will do. Daylight from a window is pleasant, though bright sun can glare when you lie on your back, so angle the mat so you are not facing straight into it. In the evening, a lamp to one side is less glaring than an overhead light when you lie down and look up.

Sound

Some people like silence, some like quiet music, and some follow a video or our sequence timer on a phone or laptop. If you use a screen, set it at the front of the mat where you can see it from standing and from the floor, and turn off notifications for the length of the session.

A corner, if you have one

If you can leave a mat rolled in a basket with blocks and a blanket beside it, starting a session takes seconds. If you cannot, keep everything in one bag or bin so setting up is a single trip.

Gear checklist: essential and optional

You can start with almost nothing and add props as you discover which poses you want them for. Our Props & Modifications guide explains what each one does and how to choose it.

Essential

  • A non-slip surface: a yoga mat, or a low-pile rug on a non-slip pad.
  • Clothes you can bend and fold in, without a waistband that digs in when you fold forward.
  • A clear stretch of wall.
  • A sturdy chair without wheels, needed for the Desk Reset and for chair versions of poses.
  • Water, and a phone within reach.

Optional

  • Two blocks, or two firm pillows or stacks of books.
  • A strap, or a belt or bathrobe tie.
  • A folded blanket or a large towel.
  • A bolster, or two bed pillows in one pillowcase.
  • A layer to put on for the final rest.

A wall and chair setup

Setting your mat against a wall, with a chair close by, puts every supported version in our pose guides within one step, without moving furniture mid-session. Here is the layout in words, as if you were looking down on the room from the ceiling.

  1. Wall at the top. Lay the short end of your mat flush against the baseboard, so the mat runs straight out into the room.
  2. Chair at the top left or right. Stand the chair beside the wall end of the mat, its back against the same wall, seat facing into the room and legs on the floor rather than on the mat.
  3. Clear floor around the rest. Leave about 3 ft along both long sides of the mat and about 3 ft beyond the far end.
  4. Props in the opposite top corner. Stack blocks, strap and blanket on the floor beside the wall end of the mat, on the side away from the chair.
  5. Screen or printout at the wall. Lean a printed sequence against the wall, or set a phone or laptop on the floor beside the props, where you can see it standing and lying down.

With this layout, Half Dog at the wall is one step forward, Tree Pose with a hand on the wall is a quarter turn, and Chair Dog is one step to the side. For floor work, turn around so your head is at the wall end and your feet point into the room.

Before you lean on anything

  • Push the chair seat toward the wall with your hands to check it cannot slide before you put weight on it.
  • Check the wall is clear of hooks, frames and shelves at head and hand height.
  • Do not use a door, a bookcase or anything on casters as your wall or chair.

A sturdy, armless chair. In the layout above, push its back flat against the wall before you use it.

Making time for practice

A practice you do in ten minutes on a Tuesday counts for more than an hour you keep meaning to find. Here is how to make room for it without rearranging your life.

Anchor it to something you already do

Rather than finding a new slot in the day, attach practice to something that already happens: after you start the coffee, after you take off your work shoes, before you brush your teeth at night. The existing routine becomes the reminder.

  • Morning: roll out the mat while the kettle heats and do the 10-Minute Morning before you eat.
  • Midday: the 8-minute Desk Reset, in a sturdy chair at your desk.
  • Evening: the 20-minute Evening Wind-Down once the dishes are done.
  • Weekend: the 40-minute Gentle Full Practice when the house is quiet.

Ten minutes counts

Short sessions are easier to start and easier to repeat. Ten minutes a few days a week gives you time to learn the poses and notice how each one feels in your body. If you have more time, take a longer practice; if you have less, do the first few poses and stop. A short session is a complete session.

Plan the week, loosely

Pick two or three days and write a sequence name next to each on a calendar. If you miss one, do the next one. There is nothing to catch up on.

Sharing space with kids or pets

Most home practice happens around other people and animals. A few ground rules keep everyone's hands, paws and feet where they belong.

Yes, with a few ground rules. Give them their own mat or towel beside yours rather than sharing one, so nobody steps on a hand. Children copy shapes quickly but tend to bounce into them, so keep their versions playful and short, and skip anything that puts weight on the head or neck. Our sequences are written for adults; let children join for the parts they enjoy and wander off when they are done.

Choose standing or seated poses where you can see the whole room, keep your eyes open, and save lying down with your eyes closed for when another adult is watching them. Ten minutes during a nap is a complete session.

Most pets are curious about anything new at floor level. Give them their own spot, a bed or a folded blanket, a few feet away, and offer a chew or toy as you begin. If they keep walking underneath you in Downward-Facing Dog, close the door for the session; a sudden bump can knock you off balance.

Mostly for grip. Shake the mat out before you start and wipe it with a damp cloth if hair builds up. Store it rolled and off the floor between sessions.

Agree on a practice time with the people you live with, and keep a basket or bin for whatever needs to come off the floor. Clearing the 3 ft around the mat then takes about a minute.

Safety at home

Practicing alone means nobody else is checking the room for you. These habits take a minute and make the space trustworthy every time you step onto it.

  • Clear the floor. Look for cords, toys, slippers and pet bowls within 3 ft of the mat before every session.
  • Use a non-slip surface. Practice barefoot on a mat or a grippy rug, not in socks on a bare floor.
  • Keep a phone within reach if you practice alone, especially for balance poses and if getting up and down from the floor is new to you.
  • Come up slowly after lying down or folding forward, with a hand on the chair or wall if you need it.
  • Stop with pain. Sharp, pinching or electric sensations, numbness, dizziness or shortness of breath are all signals to stop and rest, and to check with a qualified healthcare professional if they persist.
  • Keep it warm and bright enough that you are not rushing and can see the floor around you.

Printable setup checklist

Print this page and keep the checklist by your mat, or tick through it on screen before each session.

  • Mat or non-slip rug on a clean, level floor
  • About 3 ft of clear floor around the mat
  • Arms overhead clear of the ceiling, lights and fan (fan off)
  • Short end of the mat against a clear wall
  • Sturdy chair without wheels, back against the wall
  • Props within reach: blocks or books, strap or belt, blanket or towel
  • Phone within reach, notifications off
  • Water nearby and a layer for the final rest
